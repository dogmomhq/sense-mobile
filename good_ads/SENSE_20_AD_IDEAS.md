# SENSE — 20 New Ad Concepts

## What actually draws people to Sense (the psychology under the good ads)

Every winner in the good_ads folder works because it stacks the same five pulls:

1. **The competence itch.** Everyone believes they know animals. A moose vs elk question makes the viewer answer *involuntarily* in the first 2 seconds — they're already playing before they know it's an ad. This is the single biggest asset Sense has over every other skill game: the gameplay is legible instantly, with zero tutorial.
2. **Real stakes = real drama.** $100 on the line converts a trivia question into a story with a winner and a loser. Slot-style games can't show a loser; we can, and losers are more viral than winners.
3. **The reaction is the payoff.** The gator kid screaming with his hands on his head IS the product promise: "this is how winning feels." Faces feeling things is the most-shared content category on earth.
4. **Absurdity earns the scroll-stop.** A moose in your garage, a capybara in a spa robe — the first frame must be a video someone would watch even if it weren't an ad.
5. **Human vs human.** "A real player, right now, is faster than you" is a challenge to identity. Bots/casino framing kills this; 1v1 framing feeds it.

**How to make it way more enticing overall:** make every ad *playable* (the viewer must be able to answer along), always show what was at risk, build recurring characters people follow (the girl, the goat, the reaction kid — a franchise, not clips), and engineer comment bait — an ad whose comments fill with "it's a CAIMAN idiot" gets free distribution forever.

## The 20 concepts

**1. The 0.1-Second Loss.** She answers correctly, celebrates — then WHO WAS FASTER bars show her opponent beat her by 0.08s. Full rage meltdown. *Why it works:* near-miss psychology is the strongest replay trigger in gaming, and loss ads read as honest — which builds the trust a real-money app desperately needs. Comments fill with "I would've won."

**2. Comment-Bait Impossible Round.** Full-screen costume-animal round (caiman vs alligator vs croc vs monitor), timer runs, and the ad ENDS before revealing the answer — closer says "answer's in the app." *Why:* unresolved questions are cognitive splinters; the comment section becomes the game. Highest organic-share ceiling of any format here.

**3. Grandma Undefeated.** 78-year-old grandma calmly taps answers while her grandson loses his mind behind her; win screen; she deadpans "send another one." *Why:* subverted-expectation champion + wholesome trash talk = crossover viral (reaches non-gamers), and it visually proves "anyone can win — it's knowledge, not reflexes."

**4. Couple Duel.** Split-screen boyfriend vs girlfriend, same question, same couch. Loser's face next to winner's scream. Caption: "loser does dishes + pays the $100." *Why:* relationship-competition content is a permanently viral genre; it also demos the challenge-a-friend loop, which is the product's real growth engine.

**5. The Cash-Out.** Screen recording: balance $690 → withdraw → bank notification appears → cut to reaction cam scream. *Why:* the #1 objection to every real-money game is "you can never actually get the money." One ad that kills that objection converts the fence-sitters every hype ad leaves behind. (Use real, defensible numbers only.)

**6. Streak on the Line.** 7-win streak flame, balance stacked, one question for the 8th — hands shaking, slow tap, win. *Why:* escalation architecture. The streak chip is already in the real UI, so it demos progression while manufacturing the highest-tension 8 seconds we can build.

**7. The Zookeeper Challenge.** Real-looking zookeeper in uniform: "I do this for a living. If I can't beat a random guy from Ohio…" — loses OR barely wins. *Why:* expert-identity bait. Every hunter, farm kid, and vet tech in the comments has to weigh in. Targets the exact audience with animal knowledge — the people most likely to deposit.

**8. FaceTime Duel.** Two friends on a call, same round, phones visible — simultaneous opposite reactions: one erupts, one collapses. *Why:* zero-sum drama in a single frame. Nothing else shows "your win is literally someone's loss" this fast, and that's the whole product.

**9. POV Encounter Series (Weekly Episodes).** Continue the moose/bear/cougar format as numbered episodes — wolf in the laundry room, elk in the drive-thru, emu at the bus stop. *Why:* serialization converts viewers into followers; followers into installs. The format is already proven in our library — this is compounding, not gambling on a new format.

**10. Bar Bet.** She slides her phone across a bar to a stranger: "hundred bucks, fastest answer." Crowd gathers, phone tilts, crowd ERUPTS on the win screen. *Why:* the crowd's reaction multiplies the single-viewer reaction — social proof and celebration in one shot, and it frames Sense as a social flex, not a solo grind.

**11. The Trash Talk.** Opponent's avatar (the cowboy goat) sends "too slow 🐌" after beating her; she re-queues furious; revenge win; she sends "🐐" back. *Why:* rage → revenge → victory is a complete 15-second story arc, and it teases social features that make the game feel alive.

**12. ASMR Round.** Macro shots: fingernail tap, ring tick, lock *thunk*, win shockwave — mixed loud, no voice at all. *Why:* satisfying/ASMR is a massive discovery niche our library completely misses; the game's real sounds are genuinely good and this flexes UI quality (which signals "real app, real money").

**13. "This Is a Real Question."** Deadpan voiceover over the capybara-in-a-spa image: "this is a real question in a real app where real people bet real money." *Why:* self-aware absurdity is the meme-account tone; it makes the app itself the joke people share, and the 68-image costume library means infinite cheap sequels.

**14. David vs #1.** "I challenged the top-ranked player in the country." Their streak: 47. Slow-motion tap race, photo finish — she wins by 0.02s. *Why:* underdog narrative + leaderboard flex. Plants "there are rankings, there is a best player, you could take them down" — aspirational stakes beyond one bet.

**15. The Recruitment.** She loses three times to the same player, gets up, drives to her genius friend's house, hands him the phone: "win my money back." He does, silently. *Why:* mercenary-friend is deeply relatable comedy, and it seeds the real behavior of handing the phone around — every device pass is a new user.

**16. Money Rain IRL.** The win screen's money rain "breaks the fourth wall" — bills start falling in her actual room; she looks up, then loses it. *Why:* the max-wow surreal transition is a pattern interrupt that rewards rewatching (did that just happen?), and rewatches are the algorithm's favorite signal.

**17. The Alarm.** 3 AM. Phone buzzes: "Your rival challenged you — $100." She sits up in bed, hair wild, answers in the dark, wins, whispers "let's go," goes back to sleep. *Why:* it dramatizes challenge notifications as events worth waking up for — sticky-product storytelling disguised as a joke.

**18. Kid Explains It To Dad.** College kid shows his dad one round; dad scoffs "that's a caribou," loses; kid wins the same question; dad demands a rematch and downloads it himself. *Why:* the download moment is IN the ad — the most persuasive CTA is watching a skeptic convert on camera.

**19. Goat Mascot: Undefeated.** The cowboy goat on his hay throne, aviators on, while a montage of reaction-cam LOSERS plays — caption "he's 312-0. cash prize if you beat the goat." *Why:* a beatable villain gives the whole brand a face and a standing challenge; boss-fight framing turns every future ad into a callback.

**20. The Stitch Trap.** Post a raw round (no branding until the last second) captioned "nobody has answered this correctly in under 3 seconds." *Why:* engineered for stitches and duets — creators farm their own reaction content off our question, and every stitch is an unpaid ad with built-in reaction, the exact thing we currently pay Veo to fake.

## Priority if you're testing this week
Highest expected value first: **1 (loss), 2 (comment bait), 5 (cash-out), 4 (couple duel), 9 (series)** — they're the five that either build trust, farm comments, or compound. The rest are creative-diversity plays to keep the account from fatiguing on one format.
